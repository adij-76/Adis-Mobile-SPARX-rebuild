## Section 1: Voice rules as Adi documented them

*Reproduced from `adi-content-writing-SKILL-updated.md` (with confirmations from `pt-article-playbook.md`). His wording kept wherever possible; tooling/workflow mechanics (file paths, repurposing pipeline, platform word counts) trimmed except where they carry voice content.*

### Author identity
Dr. Adi Jaffe, Ph.D. — PhD in Psychology and Neuroscience, UCLA. Books: *The Abstinence Myth*, *Unhooked* (readunhooked.com). Psychology Today contributor; LinkedIn; keynote speaker. Organizations: IGNTD, Catalyst Leadership Partners. Content direction: moving away from addiction-specific framing toward behavior change for high performers, leadership psychology, executive self-awareness. His addiction background is "the *laboratory* where these insights were forged, not the primary subject." Bridge line when needed: *"You don't have to be an addict for this to apply to you."*

### Core IP framing (his words)
"Every persistent behavior — bad habits, self-sabotage, leadership blind spots, compulsive patterns — is serving a hidden purpose. That purpose is the 'hook.' Attacking the behavior without identifying and resolving the hook either fails or produces substitution."

The key insight line (his instruction: "use verbatim or close to it in content"):
> "Bad habits aren't your enemy. They're signals. The question isn't how to stop them — it's what they're trying to tell you."

### Voice Rules (Non-Negotiable) — as written in the skill file

1. **Reframe before asking for change.** Always establish that the behavior makes sense before asking the reader to change it. Never open from judgment.
2. **The counterintuitive claim comes first.** Title or opening sentence should challenge what the reader thinks they already know.
3. **Client story anchors the concept.** Short, concrete, shows a hook being revealed. One story per piece is usually enough. Never a full case study — just enough to make the mechanism visceral. Name them (Melissa, Bradley, Dan) — it makes them feel real.
4. **Neuroscience explains, doesn't impress.** Use brain science to make the reader feel understood, not to demonstrate credentials.
5. **Self-compassion is strategic, not sentimental.** Frame it as necessary for change (shame reinforces behavior), not as a feel-good add-on.
6. **"Most people do X — this is a mistake"** is a recurring structural move. Use it to name the wrong approach before offering the right one.
7. **Never moralize.** No "you should," "you must," "you need to." The tone is: *here's what's actually happening, here's why, here's what works.*
8. **Adi inserts himself as a fellow human.** Don't just position him as the expert looking down. He makes himself vulnerable: "As an addiction expert... this is something I have to be mindful of myself." This is not optional — it's what makes the credibility land without alienating.
9. **"But here's the thing:"** is a reliable transition punch line. Use it when pivoting from a problem to a reframe, or from conventional wisdom to the counterintuitive truth. It signals energy shift.
10. **"And the same can work for you."** — after introducing a client story or research, pivot directly to the reader with this line or close variants. It closes the gap between case and reader.
11. **The client story bookend.** In longer pieces, introduce the client early, let the article do its work, then return to their resolution at the close. It gives the piece emotional completion.
12. **Embedded book quotes.** Set off a direct *Unhooked* quote as a block: "Bad habits are not just random behaviors; they often have a hidden purpose." This builds IP and credibility simultaneously.

### Voice Calibration — March 2026 Updates (micro-moves layered on the base rules; from Adi's own edits)

1. **Short inclusive punch lines.** After a setup or scene, add a short line that lets the reader nod: "We've all seen it." / "We've all been there." One line, standalone paragraph.
2. **Visceral, physical metaphors.** If you can make the reader feel it physically, do it. Example: "it's like fungus, or mold, and it may be slowly killing your organization."
3. **Stretch the core metaphor.** Don't just introduce the metaphor — extend it. Tax → tax interest → "will come due when you get audited... by real world performance."
4. **Plain-language translation after every research finding.** Never let a data point land alone. Follow it immediately: "That means massive turnover costs, lower performance from even (especially) your best people, and more fighting."
5. **"Think about it" challenges.** Direct mid-article energy challenges. These break the reader out of passive reading.
6. **The modeling argument.** When addressing leader behavior, name the modeling effect explicitly: "if decisions are being modeled by the leaders, why would the staff believe they can make a difference?"
7. **Name what the avoidance protects — specifically.** Don't just say someone avoids something. Name the emotional protection.
8. **Name the diagnostic gap.** Organizational dysfunction is often invisible by design: "organizations rarely identify the problem, let alone the cause."
9. **Commanding action items.** Finish action items with a directive that has no wiggle room: "You don't have to do it perfectly, but you do have to do it."
10. **Engagement close (LinkedIn only).** End LinkedIn posts with a direct invitation: "I'd love to hear from you, in the comments..."
11. **Emojis for action lists (LinkedIn only).** ✅ for action item lists on LinkedIn. Nowhere else.
12. **"Culture drift" as named consequence.** When writing about organizational patterns, use "culture drift" as the named downstream consequence. It's Adi's term — own it.

### Proven voice moves (confirmed across his article corpus, 2019–2026)

- **"Now, sure..."** — casual qualifier before a pivot to the reader. Disarming, conversational.
- **"We've all been there."** — one-line standalone paragraph. Inclusive, not preachy.
- **"Think about it." / "Think about that:"** — direct challenge to reader. Mid-article energy boost.
- **"Sound familiar?"** — recognition prompt. Forces self-identification.
- **"Surprisingly (not really)"** — parenthetical sass. Shows personality.
- **"You just... pull back."** — ellipsis for emotional pacing. The pause IS the insight.
- **"So go ahead, be daring and brave."** — warm command at close. Permission + aspiration.
- **"But here's the thing:"** — hard pivot from conventional wisdom to counterintuitive truth.
- **"What if I told you..."** — setup for a counterintuitive claim. Conversational, low-resistance entry.
- **"Most people [do X]. But this is a mistake."** — names the wrong approach first; meets the reader where they are before redirecting.
- **"How did it play out for you?"** — direct challenge forcing self-reflection right after describing the common failed approach. Accountability without judgment.
- **"In all my years..."** — credentialing through accumulated experience, not just credentials. More human than "research shows."
- **Rhetorical question ending in "...is it?"** — e.g., "Ignorance isn't sounding all that blissful now, is it?" Disarms and invites.
- **Parenthetical self-disclosure:** "(Honestly, I have no idea how I would do on this.)" — drops Adi in as a fellow human. One per piece maximum.
- **Self-deprecating humor:** "And I struggle to arrange a family vacation!" — keeps the tone from getting too earnest. One per piece.
- **"And the same can work for you."** — pivot from client or example to reader.
- **"We are all human, after all."** — closing inclusive line acknowledging shared fallibility.
- **Metaphor extension** — take the core metaphor one step further into action: "the sooner you pay, the lower the bill."

### Structural rules that carry voice (from skill file + PT playbook)

- **Opening rule:** "No preamble. No warm-up. The first 3 sentences must create an involuntary 'tell me more' response." No "Have you ever wondered...", no "In my practice...". Six proven opener types: client story cold-open; shocking research fact stated as a bold claim (not "A study found..."); visceral "you" scenario ("You know the conversation. You've rehearsed it in the shower."); inner-critic quote ("'You're so stupid. How could you mess this up again?' Sound familiar?"); scene with a friend / personal moment; personal disclosure (flag for Adi's review).
- **Bridge to reader:** acknowledge the reader isn't in the extreme scenario ("Now, sure... you probably haven't...") then pivot ("But I'd be willing to bet...") with a "Maybe it's the one with..." cascade of 3–4 recognizable scenarios.
- **Metadata escalation:** Title names the concept → Subtitle challenges the reader ("You might think avoiding hard conversations is kind. You'd be very wrong.") → Description makes it personal.
- **Headers:** action-oriented, not academic. "The Compound Interest of Silence" not "Research on Demand-Withdraw Patterns." Hooks section header is a compassionate challenge: "Why Smart, Caring People Keep Avoiding" — NOT "Common Causes of Avoidance." Closing header: "Remember This" — NOT "Conclusion" or "The Bottom Line."
- **Hooks reveal is the IP payload:** dismiss the standard advice first ("'Just have the hard conversation. Be brave. Lean in.' But if it were that simple, you'd have done it already."); define a hook in one sentence; 3 numbered hooks, each a full paragraph containing: the pattern named plainly / developmental context / why it made sense then / a line that lands in the gut. Close with: "The behavior makes sense when you trace it back... The problem is that it doesn't serve you anymore."
- **Action items:** open by naming what NOT to do ("not to white-knuckle your way through the discomfort"); each item has instruction + mechanism + micro-steps + example language the reader can use verbatim ("Say one true sentence: 'I've been holding something back.'"); at least one ends with a commanding directive.
- **Research supports the emotional arc — it does not lead it.** Story first, research mid-article. Plain-language translation immediately after any data point. Name-brand sources first (Gottman), then meta-analyses with specific numbers.
- **Frameworks invisible:** "SPARO and EAT are scaffolding. PT readers don't need the acronyms." (Note: keystone articles DO name them — they're internal/anchor pieces; consumer-facing PT pieces hide them.)
- **The 8-beat emotional arc** every successful piece moves through: Recognition ("that's me") → Validation ("we've all been there") → Alarm ("it's compounding") → Expansion ("it's not just one area") → Relief ("it's not a willpower failure") → Understanding ("this is where it came from") → Agency ("here's what to do") → Warmth ("be daring and brave").
- **Standing prohibitions:** Never use generic motivational language ("unlock your potential," "transform your life") unless ironically or subverted. No hard sell, no affiliate links. Soft one-line *Unhooked* CTA at the end only. Personal stories from Adi's life must be flagged for his review before publication.

## Section 2: Observed voice patterns

*Analyst's synthesis across the five articles. These are observations of what he actually does on the page; items not traceable to a documented rule are marked (inferred).*

### Sentence rhythm
- Long explanatory sentence followed by a very short verdict sentence. "Not neutral. Negative." (emotions-edge) / "Almost two months." / "And she fell apart anyway." (identity-grief). The short sentence is where the emotional weight lands.
- One-sentence standalone paragraphs used as beats: "We've all been there." / "That distinction is everything." / "Emotion regulation strategy." / "This is the identity hook in action."
- Sentence fragments used deliberately for emphasis: "Not junior managers. Not first-time leaders. CEOs." (imposter-loop). Anaphora in triads: "You're responding to emails. You're showing up to meetings. You're making decisions." (quiet-burnout). (inferred as a pattern; the skill file documents the short-punch but not the triads)
- Italics for the pivotal word inside a sentence: "This is burnout from *being* too hard." / "a thing you *are*." He italicizes the one word that flips the meaning. (inferred)
- Em-dashes as his primary connective for mid-sentence reframes; ellipses for emotional pacing ("You just... pull back.").

### Openings
- Cold open, no throat-clearing, every time. Either: a named client doing something specific on a specific day ("Melissa stopped gaming on a Tuesday."), a statistic framed as impossible ("Here's a statistic that should feel impossible: 71% of CEOs experience impostor syndrome."), a second-person description of the reader's own life ("You look fine. By every external measure, you're performing at capacity."), or a news scene ("Last month, a post calling out the CEO of Marks & Spencer hit one million views in days.").
- The opening always ends by turning toward the reader within a few paragraphs — the "Now, sure... but I'd be willing to bet..." bridge appears nearly verbatim in identity-grief and always-on.

### How he addresses the reader
- Direct second person, as a peer he respects: "brilliant people trapped in a loop," "smart, caring people," "smart, capable people." He compliments the reader's competence *while* challenging their behavior — the challenge is never to their intelligence, only to their strategy. (inferred)
- He absolves before he challenges: "This isn't weakness." / "You're not imagining that you're more reactive." / "Your brain didn't get this wrong (it got it right, too well)." Behavior is always reframed as a formerly-adaptive protection.
- Direct mid-article commands: "Think about it." "Write it down." "Do not reassure them. Do not redirect."
- Ends on a single engagement question aimed at this week's behavior: "Which one are you going to have this week?" / "Which of the three hooks above do you recognize in yourself — or in your culture?"

### Signature moves
- **The reframe is the product.** Every piece has one central inversion: burnout isn't hours, it's identity performance; imposter syndrome isn't confidence, it's a learned threat response; relapse isn't craving, it's grief; suppression isn't strength, it's a cognitive tax; always-on isn't dedication, it's "anxiety with a corner office."
- **Dismiss the standard advice by quoting it.** He voices the conventional fix in quotes, then knocks it down: "'Just model better boundaries.' ... But if it were that simple, it would already be done." / "Affirmations don't work because they're trying to argue with your amygdala. Your amygdala doesn't debate."
- **One extended metaphor per piece, stretched across sections:** the unpaid tax that compounds; the afterimage/homesickness/ghost (identity-grief); the background process / facade as a running program (quiet-burnout); the loop (imposter). He returns to the metaphor in the close.
- **Client story structure:** named or titled client ("Melissa," "Sarah," "Dana," "a VP of Product"), a verbatim quote of their own words in their voice, the hook traced to a developmental origin, and a resolution that lands as a one-line paradox: "She hadn't fixed her burnout by doing less. She'd fixed it by being more."
- **Personal disclosure register:** "I'll be direct: I know the imposter loop from the inside." He tells his own story in the same story-structure he uses for clients, with the same turn ("That understanding... changed everything. Not because I suddenly believed in myself more.").
- **Numbered hooks and numbered actions, always three.** Three hooks, three moves, three shifts — in every article read. Each gets a full paragraph, never a one-liner.
- **Mechanism-first teaching:** plain-language behavioral sequence first, then the research citation, then an immediate plain-language translation ("Translation: Leaders who acknowledge and process emotion actually perform better...").
- **Honest hedging as credibility:** he names counter-evidence and agrees with parts of it ("That critique is useful, and I agree with it. But it actually sharpens the point here rather than weakening it."). He concedes what's true in the conventional view before overturning it ("Much of this is good work. I've used all of it. But here's the thing: it leaves a giant hole in the middle.").
- **Redefinition by negation-pair:** "That's not weakness. That's the only sustainable leadership model there is." / "That's not softness. It's precision." / "This isn't therapy work... This is engineering work." The "not X, but Y" construction is possibly his single most frequent move. (inferred)

### Recurring vocabulary and metaphors
- Core lexicon: hook / unhooked, hidden driver, "the behavior makes sense when you trace it back," "it doesn't serve you anymore," white-knuckle, facade, performing (a self / competence / authenticity), identity fusion, threat response, "your brain learned," pattern (vs. truth): "a pattern, not a truth," compound / compounding, tax / bill / cost / pay, energy accounting, culture drift, modeling, "data, not weakness/noise," strategic (self-compassion, disclosure, awareness), "cognitive resources," afterimage, homesick(ness).
- Economic/accounting metaphors dominate: tax, bill, compound interest, budget, cost, ROI, "leaking energy," audit. Engineering/systems metaphors second: background process, running a program, offline, rewire, interrupt the loop, system. (inferred categorization)
- Neuroscience anchors used repeatedly and in plain terms: prefrontal cortex as "command center" that goes "offline"; amygdala that "doesn't debate"; the reader's brain always cast as competent and protective, never broken.

### What he never does (observed + documented)
- Never opens with warm-up, definitions, or "In today's fast-paced world" framing.
- Never moralizes ("you should/must/need to" absent from all five articles except inside quoted conventional advice he's rejecting).
- Never shames the reader or the client; never evaluates a client's behavior as a character flaw.
- Never lets a statistic sit untranslated.
- Never uses generic motivational language ("unlock your potential"-type phrasing appears nowhere).
- Never hard-sells; *Unhooked* gets one soft line at the end.
- Never presents self-compassion as softness — it's always argued as performance strategy backed by mechanism.
- Never hides uncertainty — counter-evidence gets named and integrated rather than omitted.
- Never uses exclamation points for hype; the one observed ("And I struggle to arrange a family vacation!") is self-deprecating. (inferred)

## Section 4: Concepts/frameworks referenced

One-line definitions **as used in these texts**:

- **Hook / Hooks Framework** — Adi's core IP: a hidden emotional driver that makes a persistent behavior serve a purpose; "attacking the behavior without identifying and resolving the hook either fails or produces substitution." A hook "once served a purpose that hasn't been examined since."
- **Unhooked** — his book and the state the work aims at: "Unhooked doesn't mean emotionless. It means having choice in how you respond to what you feel, rather than running an automatic response pattern."
- **The Abstinence Myth** — his earlier book (named as credential; not elaborated in these texts).
- **SPARO** — his behavior-navigation model; these texts use only the **Perceive** stage ("SPARO requires you to Perceive your actual situation before you can navigate it"); under threat/burnout the Perceive stage is skipped or distorted. The full acronym expansion is not spelled out anywhere in these seven files.
- **EAT Cycle** — "Emotion → Attention → Thinking: how your emotional state shapes what you notice and how you interpret it." Used as the loop-interruption tool (notice the emotion, notice attention narrowing, interrupt the automatic thought). Skill file: SPARO and EAT are invisible scaffolding in consumer PT pieces.
- **Identity Hook** — a hook where an environmental cue automatically activates a threat response around core identity (cue → identity threat → compensatory behavior → temporary relief → reinforcement).
- **Identity fusion** — when a repeated, emotionally loaded behavior "stops being a thing you do and becomes a thing you *are*" (cited to social identity research; also hook #1 in always-on leaders).
- **The Imposter Loop** — the self-reinforcing cycle where imposter anxiety drives over-preparation, success reinforces the anxiety as "necessary," and the pattern becomes automatic; "a pattern, not a truth."
- **Quiet burnout** — "persistent high performance despite internal degradation"; burnout hidden by facade maintenance rather than shown as collapse.
- **Myth of the Unshakable Leader** — Adi's named myth: "the belief that real strength means never being moved by what happens around you" / that leaders have eliminated self-doubt.
- **Leadership Tax** — his named organizational cost: leader dysfunction (avoidance, burnout, imposter loop) compounding through decision quality and team psychology; extended via the tax/compound-interest metaphor.
- **Culture drift** — Adi's term for the slow, invisible, unnamed downstream consequence of modeled leader behavior cascading through an organization.
- **Avoidance tax** — the compounding cost of avoidance; captured coaching material extends it: self-knowledge/insight as "the most sophisticated form of avoidance, because naming the gap feels like working on it."
- **The stoicism hook** — suppression-as-identity: appearing unflappable got rewarded early, so by the C-suite "it no longer feels like a choice — it feels like identity."
- **Control anxiety** — always-on hook #2: staying available because "not knowing what's happening is unbearable," traced to early environments where vigilance was the only safety.
- **Worth equals output** — always-on hook #3: an old conditional-love script ("if I stop producing, I stop mattering") dressed in professional language.
- **Strategic awareness** — "what I call 'strategic awareness' — the ability to notice when they're in the loop and choose a different response."
- **Strategic disclosure** — reframing vulnerability as leadership: bounded, purposeful acknowledgment of capacity/uncertainty (not oversharing).
- **Energy accounting** — tracking facade-maintenance cost: rebalancing high-energy (performed) vs. restorative (aligned) activities.
- **Identity audit** — mapping the gap between performed identity and experienced reality; "This isn't therapy work... This is engineering work."
- **Self-compassion as strategic** — recurring frame: self-compassion works by de-escalating the threat response so the PFC comes back online — "not because it's soft or self-indulgent."
- **Grief model of relapse** — recovery fails when people are asked "to stop a behavior without helping them grieve the self that lived inside it"; treatment = "grieve a self and build the next one."
- **Reappraisal vs. suppression** — the emotion-regulation lever: change the *meaning* of the signal rather than hiding the emotion; "Name it, specifically / Reframe the signal, not the situation / Use it, don't manage it."
- **From Struggle to Success bridge** — the named move of connecting Adi's personal history to the reader's pattern for credibility-through-humanity.
- **The 8-beat emotional arc** — Recognition → Validation → Alarm → Expansion → Relief → Understanding → Agency → Warmth; his stated formula for every successful piece.
- **Hooks-Reveal format (Template A)** — his 10-section PT article structure whose payload is the numbered-hooks section; **Listicle format (Template B)** — items each with "Why It Works / How to Do It / Things to avoid."
- **Canonical client stories** (named in the skill file as reusable IP): Linda (marijuana ↔ social anxiety, the canonical hooks example), Aliyah, Jaylen, Meredith, Michael, Tim, Usman, Mark, Carol, James, Terry, plus articles' Dan, Melissa, Sarah, Dana.
